Removing luck

The core claim: a bettor with no edge still wins sometimes, and the winner of a large pool of no-edge bettors looks exactly like a skilled one. Everything on this page exists to separate those two things. The null simulation gives the shape of luck; the permutation null gives the shape of luck given the structure of the schedule; the reliability tests ask whether the past predicts the future at all.

1 · What luck looks like

Twenty thousand zero-edge bettors, 1,000 bets each. The 95th percentile is 0.4% and the best of every hundred such bettors averages 3.1%. Any strategy returning less than that has not demonstrated anything.

2 · Team-level skill does not survive a permutation null

no detectable team-level ATS skill. Observed dispersion of team-season ATS rates is 0.01301; the permutation benchmark that reassigns ATS winners inside each game — preserving the one-winner-per-game constraint — gives 0.01521 ± 0.00085. The point estimate of skill variance is negative, so it is reported as zero rather than clipped into a claim.

  • Team-seasons in the test: 640
  • Games per team-season: 16.5
  • Implied 95% band for a team's true ATS rate: 50.0% to 50.0%

3 · Reliability

If teams had persistent ATS skill, a team's first half would predict its second half. It does not: r = -0.058 over 640 team-seasons, and year over year r = -0.035. Spearman-Brown reliability is -0.122.

5 · The evidence bar

This table is the reason gate G1 exists. An edge of 2% needs thousands of settled bets before a one-sided 95% test can see it; a 150-bet season cannot distinguish a good bettor from a lucky one, no matter how good the story is.
