What the parlay results say

Parlays multiply both the price and the error. Two −110 legs pay about +264, which needs 27.4% of tickets to win just to break even; two independent 52.4% legs win 27.4% of the time. There is no structural edge in the multiplication itself, which is why every parlay family here returns less than a single bet on the same picks — and why the same-game version is the only one where correlation can actually change the answer.

Correlation changes the price, not the edge

A book that prices same-game legs independently will mis-price them whenever the legs are correlated. The first table below is a set of hypothetical latent-correlation scenarios: its ρ column is a latent Gaussian parameter, not the binary φ measured in this data. The second table converts each measured joint probability into the latent ρ that reproduces it, so a measured pair can be priced instead of guessed. Neither table is a claim about any particular sportsbook's multiplier — those multipliers are not in the verified data and are therefore not quoted here.

How to build a same-game parlay with this

  1. Only combine legs whose measured φ is outside the noise band (|φ| > 0.054 at n = 3,400).
  2. Price the pair with the copula joint probability at the calibrated latent ρ, never the product.
  3. Cross-game combinations: the measured within-week pairwise φ is -0.005 (week-clustered 95% interval -0.014 to 0.005). That is consistent with independence but does not establish joint independence of a multi-leg ticket, so treat a cross-game parlay price as an assumption to check, not a derivation.
  4. Re-check the price against the book's own same-game multiplier once one is sourced (F3).
